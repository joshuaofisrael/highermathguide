Choose a pillar

How to choose

A pillar is a map of one subject. A pathway is an ordered route for one audience, with checkpoints you produce. Start on a pillar when you want the ideas and the links. Follow a pathway when you want the sittings. There are three pathways: an AP Calculus AB bridge, a linear algebra first course, and intro to proofs. Probability does not have a separate pathway. Its pillar is the order.

Choose by the work in front of you, not by which title sounds more advanced. If algebra and graphs are the actual gap, open calculus foundations or linear algebra and use the glossary when a word is doing real work. The FAQ on how to use the site describes the same loop: topic, explainer, glossary, calculator, then a pathway.

Real analysis and proof habits

Audience. Readers whose calculus was mostly computation, and whose next course will ask for arguments.

Differential equations first look

Audience. Readers meeting equations that relate a function to its derivative, including separable equations and slope fields.

Next step. Separate one equation on paper, then read the separable walkthrough. Constant coefficients, after that, are the second order calculator, the Wronskian calculator, and the Laplace transform calculator.

When two pillars both fit

If you can compute derivatives and freeze on "for every epsilon there exists a delta," read calculus foundations through the derivative and integral ideas, then switch to real analysis and proof habits. The intro to proofs pathway is the writing practice for that switch.

If the next course is multivariable calculus, do linear algebra through eigenvalues before multivariable and vector calculus. Jacobian matrices in plain language is the handoff. Matrices as pictures make that page shorter.

If the interesting object is a rate that depends on the current amount, stay on differential equations and the separable explainer. Series and proof habits can wait unless the course lists them. If the interesting object is a conditional probability, stay on probability and statistics. Calculus is not a gate for Bayes theorem.

Pathways, when you want an order

The pillars link into the pathways. The pathways link back. Use the pathways hub to compare audience, starting point, and time shape.

  • The AP Calculus AB bridge assumes you already differentiate and integrate, and it orders meaning, series, and a first differential equation. It is not an official exam review.
  • The linear algebra first course orders vectors, matrices, eigenvalues, and the Jacobian handoff.
  • Intro to proofs orders epsilon delta, sequences, and series tests as writing practice.

A full book or course notes, when a pillar stops on purpose, is on the sources page. Questions about the study loop are on the FAQ.

Common questions

Which topic should I open first?

Open the pillar that matches the course in front of you. Calculus foundations if change and accumulation are the gap. Linear algebra if matrices are the gap. Probability and statistics if chance or data are the gap. Real analysis if proofs are the gap. Multivariable calculus and differential equations usually come after a first calculus course. If you are unsure, pick the page whose first checkpoint you can actually do this week.

How is a topic pillar different from a pathway?

A pillar is a cluster: the ideas, the outcomes, the pitfalls, and a suggested order into pages that already exist. A pathway is a longer ordered route for one audience, with checkpoints you produce. There are three pathways. Start on a pillar for the map. Follow a pathway when you want the sittings.

Do these pages replace a textbook?

No. Each pillar says what to hold in mind and which lesson on this site to open next. A full development lives in an open book or course notes. The sources page lists OpenStax, MIT OpenCourseWare, and other references used across the site.
