Multivariable and vector calculus

Topic pillar · Educational only · Updated 23 Sep 2026

Once a quantity depends on more than one input, single variable calculus is only the local tool. Height can depend on two map coordinates. Temperature in a room depends on three. A map can send a point in the plane to another point in the plane. Multivariable calculus extends derivatives and integrals to several inputs. Vector calculus organizes those ideas when the output, or the domain, lives in the plane or in space. This pillar is a first look map: who it helps, what the core pictures are, and which pages already on this site to open in order.

Who this pillar is for

Start here if a course in several variables is in front of you, or if partial derivative symbols have appeared and the pictures have not. You should already be able to differentiate and integrate in one variable, and to say what a derivative measures. If that sentence is shaky, repair it on calculus foundations and what a derivative actually measures before you add inputs.

If matrices still feel like grids to grind, do the linear algebra first course first. Gradients, Jacobians, and divergence are much shorter once a matrix is a geometric machine. This page assumes you want the map of ideas. The pathway is the ordered route through the linear algebra pages that make the later steps possible.

Prerequisites

You need single variable limits, derivatives, and a picture of the definite integral as signed accumulation. You need coordinates in the plane. Dot products help immediately: a positive dot product means a similar direction, zero means a right angle (or a zero vector), and a negative dot product means an obtuse angle.

Read partial derivative, gradient, vector, and vector field when you want the short form of a word. OpenStax Calculus and MIT OpenCourseWare are on the sources page if you want a full development of line integrals and the big theorems. This pillar will not replace that book. It will tell you which idea to hold while you read it.

What you should be able to do

When you finish the core, you should be able to do these things in a sentence, then on a small example.

  • Compute a partial derivative by holding the other inputs fixed, and say which input was allowed to move.
  • Build a gradient and say that it points toward steepest increase, perpendicular to a level curve.
  • Write the linear approximation in several variables, and name a Jacobian as the matrix of that approximation when there are several outputs.
  • Set up a constrained optimization as "gradients parallel," and know where to read the method: Lagrange multipliers intuition.
  • Distinguish a scalar field, a vector field, divergence, and curl in words, and state what Stokes theorem equates.

Partial derivatives and the gradient

A partial derivative holds every variable but one fixed and differentiates with respect to the free one. For $z=f(x,y)$, the symbol $\partial f/\partial x$ asks how $z$ changes when $x$ moves and $y$ stays put. It is an ordinary derivative along a line parallel to the $x$ axis. The other partial derivative is the ordinary derivative along a line parallel to the $y$ axis.

The gradient $\nabla f$ packages the first partials into one vector. Under the usual Euclidean geometry it points in the direction of steepest increase, and its length is that steepest slope. Walking along a level curve keeps $f$ constant, so the gradient is perpendicular to the level curve. Walking with the gradient changes $f$ fastest.

Picture. Let $f(x,y)=x^{2}y$. Then $\partial f/\partial x = 2xy$ and $\partial f/\partial y = x^{2}$. At $(1,2)$ the gradient is $(4,1)$. Moving a little in the direction of $(4,1)$ increases $f$ faster than moving the same distance in any other direction. Moving perpendicular to $(4,1)$ keeps $f$ nearly constant, which is the level curve idea in a single point.

Linear approximation and the Jacobian

Near a point where the partial derivatives behave well, a scalar function looks like its tangent plane:

$$f(\mathbf{x}) \approx f(\mathbf{a}) + \nabla f(\mathbf{a})\cdot(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{a}).$$

That is the several variable cousin of $f(a)+f'(a)(x-a)$. The dot product with the gradient is the whole first order correction.

When the output is itself a vector, the derivative is a matrix. The Jacobian is the matrix of all first partial derivatives. Its columns say how the output moves when you nudge one input at a time. For a square map, the determinant of that matrix scales local area. A zero determinant means the approximation flattens area, so nearby outputs are squeezed onto a thinner set.

Read Jacobian matrices in plain language for the full story. A small check you can do before that page: let $F(x,y)=(x+y,\, xy)$. At $(1,1)$ every partial derivative equals $1$, so the Jacobian is $\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}$. The determinant is $0$. The two columns are the same vector, so the linear approximation collapses the plane onto the line through $(1,1)$. Confirm the determinant on the 2 by 2 matrix calculator after you have written the matrix yourself. That calculator multiplies and finds determinants. It does not compute partial derivatives.

Constrained optimization

An unconstrained local maximum in the interior occurs where the gradient is the zero vector. A constraint $g(x,y)=c$ leaves only the directions tangent to that curve. At a constrained max or min, $\nabla f$ is parallel to $\nabla g$, written $\nabla f = \lambda \nabla g$ when $\nabla g$ is not zero. The number $\lambda$ measures how the optimal value responds if you nudge the allowed level of $g$. Read Lagrange multipliers intuition once gradients are comfortable, with the Lagrange multiplier glossary line.

A geometry check, separate from the explainer's product example: the point on the line $x+y=1$ closest to the origin minimizes $f(x,y)=x^{2}+y^{2}$. Then $\nabla f=(2x,2y)$ and $\nabla g=(1,1)$ for $g=x+y$. Parallelism forces $x=y$, and the line forces $x=y=1/2$. The closest point is the place where the growing circle $x^{2}+y^{2}=r^{2}$ first touches the line, so the normals agree. That touch is the picture behind the algebra.

Vector fields, divergence, curl, and Stokes

A vector field assigns a vector to each point: wind, fluid velocity, or a force. A scalar field assigns a number. Writing "grad," "div," and "curl" in words is often clearer than a page of $\nabla$ symbols while you learn.

Divergence measures local expansion or compression. Curl measures local rotation. A line integral asks how much the field pushes along a path. A flux integral asks how much crosses a surface. Stokes theorem equates circulation around the oriented boundary of a surface with the flux of the curl through the surface. Green's theorem is the flat special case. Stokes theorem intuition works one example and explains why a kinder surface with the same rim gives the same circulation.

The vector operations calculator checks a dot product, a cross product, a magnitude, or a unit vector. A cross product's length is the area of the parallelogram the inputs span. The page does not compute a curl.

Suggested learning order

Work in this order. If a step uses a word you cannot define, stop and read the glossary line before the next explainer.

  1. If a derivative is still only a rule, refresh calculus foundations and what a derivative measures.
  2. If matrices are new, follow the linear algebra first course through eigenvalues before the Jacobian.
  3. Glossary: partial derivative and gradient. Compute $\nabla(x^{2}y)$ at $(1,2)$. You should get $(4,1)$.
  4. Jacobian matrices in plain language. Write a 2 by 2 Jacobian, then check its determinant on the matrix calculator.
  5. Lagrange multipliers with constraint. Do the closest point on $x+y=1$ as a second check.
  6. Vector field, then Stokes theorem intuition. State the theorem in one sentence before the worked circle.
  7. A full exercise set is on the sources page. This order is the cluster on this site.

Checkpoints and practice habits

Practice habit. Every time you differentiate in several variables, write the sentence "I am letting this input move, and holding these inputs fixed." If you cannot name the frozen inputs, the partial derivative is not finished.

Gradient check. For $f(x,y)=x^{2}+y^{2}$, the gradient is $(2x,2y)$, which points straight away from the origin. Level curves are circles. The radius is perpendicular to the circle. That is the gradient picture on the simplest hill.

Jacobian check. For $F(x,y)=(x+y,\, xy)$ at $(1,1)$, the Jacobian is $\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}$ and the determinant is $0$. Confirm the determinant on the matrix calculator after the matrix is on your paper.

Dot product check. The vectors $(4,1)$ and $(1,-4)$ have dot product $4\cdot 1 + 1\cdot(-4) = 0$, so they are perpendicular. Use the vector operations calculator only to confirm the zero. A zero dot product is the algebraic form of "this direction stays on the level curve to first order."

Common pitfalls

Pitfall. A partial derivative is a derivative along one coordinate direction. If you forget which input was held fixed, the number can be right and the meaning wrong. The Jacobian determinant scales area for the linear approximation. Area and volume integrals still need their own setup.

Lagrange multipliers find candidates. You still compare values, and you still watch places where $\nabla g$ vanishes. A sign error in Stokes theorem is usually an orientation error. The Stokes explainer treats that with care.

Common questions

Do I need linear algebra before this pillar?

You can meet partial derivatives with only single variable calculus. Gradients, Jacobians, and vector operations get much shorter if you already read a matrix as a geometric machine. The linear algebra first course is the orderly way to get that picture. Do it before the Jacobian explainer if columns and determinants are still new.

What is the difference between a gradient and a Jacobian?

A gradient belongs to a scalar function, one number out. It is the vector of first partial derivatives and points toward steepest increase. A Jacobian belongs to a map that may have several outputs. It is the matrix of all first partial derivatives, and it is the best linear approximation to that map at a point.

When should I study Lagrange multipliers?

After you can compute a gradient and say that it is perpendicular to a level curve. Lagrange multipliers find candidates where the gradient of the objective is parallel to the gradient of the constraint. Read the explainer once those two sentences are easy, and use the glossary entries for gradient, constraint, and Lagrange multiplier.

Is Stokes theorem a different subject from single variable calculus?

It is the same idea of a fundamental theorem, moved to curves and surfaces. Circulation around the oriented boundary of a surface equals the flux of the curl through the surface. Green's theorem is the flat special case. Read the Stokes explainer after you can say what a line integral and a curl are asking.

Which calculator on this site helps with several variables?

The vector operations calculator checks a dot product, a cross product, a magnitude, or a unit vector. The 2 by 2 matrix calculator checks a determinant or a product after you have written a Jacobian by hand. Neither tool computes partial derivatives for you.
