Stretch factors

2 by 2 eigenvalue calculator

Calculator · Runs offline in your browser · Educational only · Updated 18 Sep 2026

This tool finds eigenvalues of a single $2\times 2$ real matrix $A=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix}$. Eigenvalues are the roots of the characteristic polynomial $\lambda^2-(\mathrm{tr}\,A)\lambda+\det A=0$, where $\mathrm{tr}\,A=a+d$ and $\det A=ad-bc$.

The roots are meaningful when you want the stretch factors along directions that $A$ does not tip. A real eigenvalue $\lambda$ means some nonzero vector $\mathbf{v}$ satisfies $A\mathbf{v}=\lambda\mathbf{v}$. A complex conjugate pair means the real plane map rotates and scales, with no real invariant line.

This is not a full eigensolver. It does not return eigenvectors, it does not handle $3\times 3$ matrices, and it does not prove diagonalizability. It solves a quadratic. For the geometry, read the eigenvalue explainer.

Matrix entries

How to use the characteristic polynomial

Enter the four entries $a,b,c,d$. The default $\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}$ has trace $4$ and determinant $3$, so the polynomial is $\lambda^2-4\lambda+3=0$ and the roots are $3$ and $1$. Press Find eigenvalues and confirm those two numbers.

The page reports the trace, the determinant, the discriminant of the quadratic, and the roots. Use that audit line. If you only stare at the $\lambda$ list, you miss the arithmetic that produced it.

How to read the output

Two distinct real roots mean two real stretch factors. A repeated real root means a double algebraic eigenvalue; the geometry may or may not have two independent eigenvectors, and this page will not decide that.

A negative discriminant produces $\alpha\pm\beta i$. Those are still eigenvalues over $\mathbb{C}$. Over $\mathbb{R}$ you should picture a rotation scaling rather than two real invariant lines.

The sum of the eigenvalues equals the trace. The product equals the determinant. If a reported pair fails those two checks, something is off. On a healthy run they should match.

Common pitfalls

A repeated root is numerically fragile. A discriminant that should be zero may print as a tiny positive or negative number, splitting one eigenvalue into two close values or into a tiny imaginary part. If you expected a perfect square, check the entries.

Eigenvalues are not entries of $A$ except in special cases (triangular matrices, for example). Do not read $a$ and $d$ as the spectrum unless you know the matrix is triangular.

Without eigenvectors you cannot yet diagonalize, and you cannot yet say which direction stretches. The number $\lambda$ is the factor. The vector is the direction. This page only has the factor.

Common questions

What is an eigenvalue in this tool?

A number lambda such that A v equals lambda v for some nonzero vector v. This page finds those lambda values for a 2 by 2 matrix by solving the characteristic polynomial. It does not find v.

What if the eigenvalues are complex?

The real matrix still has a real characteristic polynomial, so nonreal roots come in a conjugate pair. Over the reals the map rotates and scales. Over the complex numbers those roots are ordinary eigenvalues.

Where are the eigenvectors?

Not here. Once you have lambda, you solve (A minus lambda I) v equals 0 by hand or with a later tool. The explainer shows what that direction means.

How can I check the answer quickly?

The two eigenvalues should sum to the trace a plus d and multiply to the determinant ad minus bc. If those two checks fail, do not trust the list.
