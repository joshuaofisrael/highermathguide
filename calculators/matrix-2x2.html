Small linear maps

2 by 2 matrix calculator

By the Higher Math Guide editorial team

Calculator · Runs offline in your browser · Educational only · Updated 18 Sep 2026

Educational tool; results are estimates for learning, verify with your textbook or instructor.

This toolkit works with two $2\times 2$ matrices $A$ and $B$. You can ask for a determinant, an inverse when it exists, or the products $AB$ and $BA$. Every entry is an ordinary number.

The numbers are meaningful when you are studying a linear map on the plane, a $2\times 2$ Jacobian, or a tiny system $A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}$. In those settings the determinant tells you about oriented area scaling and invertibility, and the products show that order matters.

This is not a computer algebra system for $n\times n$ matrices. It does not compute eigenvalues on this page, it does not row reduce a general system, and it does not prove a theorem. For stretch factors, use the eigenvalue tool. For the idea of a derivative matrix, read the Jacobian explainer.

How to use the toolkit

Fill the four entries of $A$ and, if you need products or a second determinant, the four entries of $B$. The default $A$ is $\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\3&4\end{pmatrix}$, whose determinant is $-2$. Choose an operation from the menu and press Compute.

Determinant of $A$ is the fastest self check. Inverse of $A$ uses the adjugate formula $(1/\det A)\begin{pmatrix}d&-b\\-c&a\end{pmatrix}$. Product $AB$ multiplies in that order. Product $BA$ reverses the order on purpose so you can see they need not match.

How to read the output

A determinant is a single number. If it is zero, $A$ is singular: columns are linearly dependent, the map collapses area, and no inverse exists. If it is nonzero, the inverse option should return a matrix that multiplies with $A$ to something near the identity.

Products print as a $2\times 2$ array. Compare $AB$ and $BA$ on the default matrices. They disagree, which is the whole lesson. Matrix multiplication is composition of maps, and composition is not commutative in general.

Common pitfalls

A determinant near zero is a warning, not a rounding trophy. The inverse formula divides by $\det A$. If that number is tiny, entries of $A^{-1}$ can explode, and floating point will look confident while being fragile.

Do not read $AB$ as “$A$ and then $B$” without fixing a convention. Here $AB$ means $A$ acts after $B$ on a column vector, the usual textbook order. If your course writes maps on the left, this is the matching product.

The page only knows $2\times 2$ arrays. A Jacobian of a map $\mathbb{R}^2\to\mathbb{R}^2$ fits. A $3\times 3$ rotation does not. Do not paste a larger matrix into four boxes and hope.

A 3 by 3 determinant and inverse, with each minor written out in exact fractions, is on the 3 by 3 matrix calculator.

To row reduce a system and read whether it has one solution, infinitely many, or none, use the reduced row echelon form calculator.

Common questions

What does the determinant measure here?

For a 2 by 2 real matrix it is the signed area scale factor of the linear map. Zero means the map flattens the plane onto a line or a point, so no inverse exists.

When does the inverse fail?

When the determinant is zero, or so close to zero that the page treats it as singular. That is not a software bug. A singular matrix has no inverse.

Why can AB and BA disagree?

Multiplication is composition. Doing B then A is a different map from doing A then B, except in special cases. Compute both on the default matrices and look at the two arrays.

Does this page find eigenvalues?

No. Use the 2 by 2 eigenvalue calculator for the characteristic polynomial. This page stays with determinant, inverse, and products.
