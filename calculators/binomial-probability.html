Independent trials

Binomial probability calculator

Calculator · Runs offline in your browser · Educational only · Updated 18 Sep 2026

This tool evaluates a binomial model $X\sim\mathrm{Binomial}(n,p)$. You enter the number of trials $n$, the number of successes $k$, and a success probability $p$. It reports $C(n,k)$ and $P(X=k)=C(n,k)\,p^k(1-p)^{n-k}$.

The number is meaningful when you really have $n$ independent trials, each with the same success probability $p$, and you care about exactly $k$ successes. Coin tosses, yes or no items on a checklist, and other two outcome repeats can fit. Updating a belief after seeing data is a different job: that is Bayes theorem.

This is not a proof of a probability axiom, and it is not a general statistics package. It does not compute a cumulative tail unless you add terms yourself, and it does not fit $p$ from data. It evaluates one point of one model.

How to use the model

Enter integers $n$ and $k$ with $0\le k\le n$, and a $p$ in $[0,1]$. The default $n=10$, $k=3$, $p=0.5$ is ten fair coin tosses and the chance of exactly three heads. Press Compute.

The first piece $C(n,k)$ counts the ways to place $k$ successes among $n$ trials. The second piece is the probability of one specific pattern of $k$ successes and $n-k$ failures, multiplied by that count.

How to read the output

Read $P(X=k)$ as a point mass, not as “at least $k$” and not as “at most $k$”. If a homework prompt asks for a tail, you must sum several $k$ values. This page will not do that sum for you.

Probabilities use log space arithmetic so moderate $n$ does not overflow as often as a naive $n!$ would. You may still see $0$ for a probability that is smaller than a double can hold. That zero means “too small to represent”, not a proof that the event is impossible in the model.

Common pitfalls

Independence and a constant $p$ are assumptions, not decorations. If later trials depend on earlier ones, or if $p$ drifts, the binomial formula is the wrong model even when the arithmetic is perfect.

$n$ is capped at $1000$ on this page. That is plenty for homework and still finite. Do not treat a capped tool as a statement about infinite populations.

$C(n,k)$ can be a huge integer. The printed value is a floating point number, so very large counts may be rounded. The probability next to it is usually the quantity you wanted anyway.

Common questions

Is P(X equals k) a tail probability?

No. It is the probability of exactly k successes. A phrase such as at least k needs a sum over several k values. Add those runs yourself.

When is the binomial model the wrong tool?

When trials are not independent, when p is not constant, or when there are more than two outcomes. The formula can still print a number. The number will not answer the real experiment.

Why can the probability print as zero?

Floating point underflow. For large n and a k far from the mean, the true probability can be smaller than a double can store. Read that zero as too small to represent, not as a logic proof.

What is C(n, k) doing in the formula?

It counts unordered ways to choose which k of the n trials are the successes. Each such pattern has the same probability. Multiply the count by that shared probability.
