Convergence tests for series without fog

Explainer · Educational only · Updated 17 Sep 2026

An infinite series is not a long plus sign. It is a sequence of partial sums. The series $\sum a_n$ converges when $s_N=a_1+\cdots+a_N$ approaches a number. Tests exist because you often cannot write $s_N$ in closed form. A good test answers only one question. Do those partial sums settle?

This page treats three workhorses: comparison, ratio, and root. They are cousins, not rivals. Comparison borrows a series you already understand. Ratio and root ask whether your terms eventually look geometric. For the broader story of sequences and series, start with sequences, series, and when infinite sums make sense. For proof habits around limits, see real analysis and proof habits.

A reminder you will need

If $a_n$ does not go to $0$, the series diverges. That is the term test. It never proves convergence. Terms can go to zero slowly and the sum can still run to infinity. The harmonic series $\sum 1/n$ is the famous example. Terms vanish. Partial sums grow like $\ln N$.

Positive term series are the cleanest setting for the three tests below. If terms change sign, ask first about absolute convergence, $\sum |a_n|$. Absolute convergence implies ordinary convergence. The converse can fail. Alternating series have their own test, which this page leaves to a later note.

Comparison: sandwich against a known series

Suppose $0\le a_n\le b_n$ for all large $n$. If $\sum b_n$ converges, then $\sum a_n$ converges. The partial sums of $a$ are increasing and bounded above by a convergent pile plus a finite head, so they settle. If instead $a_n\ge c_n\ge 0$ and $\sum c_n$ diverges, then $\sum a_n$ diverges. You cannot be smaller than a divergent stack of nonnegative blocks and still stay finite.

The usual comparison partners are geometric series $\sum r^n$ (converges when $|r|<1$) and $p$ series $\sum 1/n^p$ (converges when $p>1$). You must actually check the inequality, or a limit form of it, not only the “looks like” feeling.

Example For $a_n=\frac{1}{n^2+3n+1}$, note that $n^2+3n+1>n^2$, so $0<a_n<1/n^2$. The $p$ series with $p=2$ converges, so this series converges. The inequality is doing the work, not the visual resemblance alone.

Limit comparison is the same idea with less inequality pain. If $a_n>0$, $b_n>0$, and $a_n/b_n\to L$ with $0<L<\infty$, then $\sum a_n$ and $\sum b_n$ converge or diverge together. The terms are eventually the same size up to a constant factor. Constants do not change convergence of a series of positive terms.

Pitfall. Comparing with a smaller convergent series proves nothing. Being below a finite sum is helpful. Being above a finite sum is not a crime. Likewise, being below a divergent series does not force divergence. Match the direction of the inequality to the claim you want.

Ratio: ask if the terms become geometric

For $a_n\neq 0$, form $L=\lim |a_{n+1}/a_n|$ when the limit exists. The ratio test says:

  • If $L<1$, the series converges absolutely.
  • If $L>1$, or if the ratio tends to infinity, the series diverges.
  • If $L=1$, the test is silent.

Why $L<1$ wins: eventually $|a_{n+1}|\le r|a_n|$ for some $r$ with $L<r<1$. From there on, the tail is dominated by a geometric series with ratio $r$. Comparison finishes the proof. If $L>1$, terms grow and cannot go to zero.

The silent case $L=1$ includes both $\sum 1/n$ and $\sum 1/n^2$. The ratio test cannot see the difference between $p=1$ and $p=2$. That is not a defect in you. It is a limit of the test. Switch to comparison or an integral test.

Example For $a_n=n^2/3^n$,

$$\frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n}=\frac{(n+1)^2}{n^2}\cdot\frac{1}{3}\to\frac{1}{3}<1.$$

The polynomial in the numerator is helpless against the exponential. The series converges. Factorials in a denominator produce the same verdict for many Taylor remainders.

Root: a closer cousin of geometric series

Form $\rho=\lim\sup\sqrt[n]{|a_n|}$, or an ordinary limit when it exists. The root test uses the same three way split as the ratio test, with $\rho$ in place of $L$. The slogan is even closer to geometry. If the $n$th root settles below $1$, then $|a_n|\le r^n$ eventually for some $r<1$.

Root is often stronger than ratio in the official sense that it can decide some series where the ratio limit fails to exist. In a first course you will meet it most often with expressions already raised to the $n$th power, such as $\bigl(\frac{n}{n+1}\bigr)^{n^2}$ style terms, where the root simplifies the algebra.

When both limits exist, they are equal. You may choose whichever quotient or root looks cleaner. Silence at $1$ is still silence.

A small decision map

  1. Do terms fail to go to zero? Diverge. Stop.
  2. Do you see a geometric ratio or a $p$ series after a simple bound? Compare.
  3. Do you see $n!$, $c^n$, or a rational function times an exponential? Try ratio.
  4. Do you see an $n$th power wrapping the whole term? Try root.
  5. If the test returns $1$, change tools. Do not stare at the same limit.

Finite arithmetic and geometric sums are not the same as infinite series tests. The sequence and series calculator reports $a_n$ and $S_n$ for finite $n$. That is a numerical window, not a proof of convergence. Watching $S_n$ level off can support a guess. It cannot replace a test.

Related glossary terms: series, comparison test, ratio test, root test. The AP Calculus AB bridge lists series as an optional next step after derivatives and integrals.
