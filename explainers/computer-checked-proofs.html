What does a computer checked proof actually prove?

By the Higher Math Guide editorial team. Last reviewed: 5 November 2026.

Explainer · Educational only · Updated 5 Nov 2026

Short answer. A proof checked by a proof assistant such as Lean guarantees that a precisely written formal statement follows from the definitions and axioms it uses, with every step verified by a small trusted program called the kernel. It does not guarantee that the formal statement says what a human meant. That gap is where careful reading still matters.

Four things that are easy to blur

  1. The theorem. The idea in ordinary mathematical language, for example "the sum of two even integers is even".
  2. The definitions and assumptions. What "even" and "integer" mean, and which axioms are allowed.
  3. The formalization. The exact statement written in the proof assistant's language.
  4. The proof. The steps, which the software checks one by one.

The software is responsible for item 4. People are responsible for checking that items 2 and 3 really capture item 1.

A tiny worked example

On paper: if $a=2m$ and $b=2n$ for integers $m$ and $n$, then $a+b=2(m+n)$, and $m+n$ is an integer, so $a+b$ is even.

In a proof assistant, you would state something like "for all integers $a$ and $b$, if $a$ is even and $b$ is even, then $a+b$ is even", using the library's definition of even. The checker then confirms each step. If someone had accidentally written the statement for natural numbers instead of integers, or used a different meaning of "even", the checker would still happily confirm it. The proof would be correct, but it would prove a slightly different claim.

What the check does guarantee

  • No step was skipped or hand waved. Every inference is checked mechanically.
  • The trusted part is small. You trust the kernel and the axioms, not the long proof script or the person who wrote it.
  • Reviewers can focus their attention on the statement and definitions instead of thousands of lines of steps.

What it does not guarantee

  • That the statement is the intended one. A mis stated theorem can be proved perfectly.
  • That library definitions match the textbook. Definitions in a library such as Mathlib are carefully reviewed, but you should still know which one is in use.
  • That no extra axioms were added. Proof assistants can report which axioms a result depends on, and that list is worth checking.

How to read a claim about a formalized result

When you see a headline that a major theorem has been formalized, ask three questions. Has the formal statement been published and reviewed by people outside the team? Which definitions and axioms does it rely on? Has the proof been checked by an independent build, not only described? Until those answers are public, treat the result as a claim. The habit is the same one our epsilon delta guide teaches: read the statement exactly before trusting the argument.

Common questions

Does a Lean proof mean the theorem is true?

It means the formal statement follows from the formal definitions and axioms, as checked by Lean's kernel. Whether that statement matches the theorem people mean is a separate, human question.

Can a computer checked proof be wrong?

The checking itself is very reliable, but the statement can be mis formalized, a definition can differ from the textbook one, or extra axioms can be assumed. Those are where reviewers look.

Do I need to read the whole proof to trust it?

No. The point of a proof assistant is that you mainly need to read and trust the statement and the definitions it uses, not every step.
