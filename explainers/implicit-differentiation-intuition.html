Implicit differentiation intuition

Explainer · Educational only · Updated 17 Sep 2026

Some curves arrive as an equation that mixes $x$ and $y$, not as a formula $y=f(x)$. A circle $x^2+y^2=1$ is the usual first picture. You can solve for $y$ on the upper half, then differentiate. Implicit differentiation skips that solve. It treats $y$ as a function of $x$ that the equation already constrains, then differentiates both sides.

The method looks like a trick until you hear the promise. If a smooth piece of the curve really is the graph of some $y(x)$, then every identity that holds along that piece remains an identity after you differentiate with respect to $x$. The derivative $y'(x)$ is the unknown slope. Algebra then isolates it.

What implicit is naming

Explicit means you are given $y$ as a recipe in $x$. Implicit means you are given a relation $F(x,y)=0$ and you trust that, near a point you care about, there is a unique smooth $y(x)$ hiding inside that relation. You do not need the recipe in hand. You need the relation, the chain rule, and a point where the curve is not vertical.

That last clause is the geometric warning. If the tangent is vertical, $y$ is not a function of $x$ in any neighborhood. You can still talk about $dx/dy$, or you can stay with a parametric description. Implicit differentiation with respect to $x$ is for pieces that pass the vertical line test locally.

The chain rule does the work

Suppose $y=y(x)$ and you meet a term like $y^2$. As a function of $x$ that is the composition $g(y(x))$ with $g(u)=u^2$. The chain rule says

$$\frac{d}{dx}\bigl(y(x)^2\bigr)=2y(x)\,y'(x).$$

People write $2y\,y'$ and keep moving. The extra $y'$ is not decoration. It is the reminder that $y$ is riding on $x$. If you differentiate $y^2$ as if $y$ were a constant, you have changed the problem into a different curve.

The same pattern hits $xy$, $\sin y$, $e^{xy}$, and any other mix. Differentiate as usual, and every time a $y$ is hit you multiply by $y'$. Then collect every term that contains $y'$ and solve. This is the same linear approximation idea as in what a derivative actually measures, just applied to a constraint instead of to a formula.

A circle you can check by hand

Example Start with $x^2+y^2=1$. Differentiate both sides with respect to $x$:

$$2x+2y\,y'=0.$$

If $y\neq 0$, then $y'=-x/y$. On the unit circle the radius to $(x,y)$ is the vector $\langle x,y\rangle$. The tangent should be perpendicular to that radius, so a tangent direction is $\langle -y,x\rangle$ or $\langle y,-x\rangle$. Slope of the first is $-x/y$ when $y\neq 0$. The implicit result matches the geometry.

At $(0,1)$ you get $y'=0$, a horizontal tangent. At $(1,0)$ the formula $y'=-x/y$ blows up, which is honest. The tangent is vertical. The upper semicircle $y=\sqrt{1-x^2}$ gives the same derivative $y'=-x/\sqrt{1-x^2}=-x/y$ on $y>0$. Implicit work saved the square root, then agreed with it.

Pitfall. After you isolate $y'$, the right hand side still contains $y$. That is expected. The slope depends on where you sit on the curve. To get a number, substitute the point that satisfies the original equation. A point that fails $x^2+y^2=1$ is not on this circle, so its slope is meaningless here.

A quieter example with a product

Example Take $x^3+xy+y^3=3$ near $(1,1)$, which satisfies the equation. Differentiate:

$$3x^2+\bigl(y+x y'\bigr)+3y^2 y'=0.$$

Collect $y'$ terms: $x y'+3y^2 y'=-3x^2-y$, so

$$y'=\frac{-3x^2-y}{x+3y^2}$$

when the denominator is not zero. At $(1,1)$ this is $-4/4=-1$. You now have a tangent line $y-1=-1\cdot(x-1)$ without solving a cubic for $y$.

When the method is allowed

The implicit function theorem, in undergraduate language, says this. If $F(x,y)$ is smooth and $F_y$ is not zero at a point on $F=0$, then near that point the level set is the graph of a unique smooth $y(x)$, and

$$y'=-\frac{F_x}{F_y}.$$

That is implicit differentiation in one line. The denominator $F_y$ is exactly the coefficient you would have solved for $y'$. If $F_y=0$ and $F_x\neq 0$, the curve is locally a graph $x=x(y)$ instead. If both vanish, the level set may cross itself or cusp, and a single tangent may fail.

You do not need the full theorem to compute homework slopes. You do need it as a sanity check. Vertical tangents, self crossings, and isolated points are not bugs in your algebra. They are places where the story “$y$ is a function of $x$” paused.

Related rates are the same idea

If $x$ and $y$ both depend on time $t$, and a constraint $F(x,y)=0$ holds for all $t$ in an interval, differentiate the constraint with respect to $t$. You obtain a linear relation between $x'(t)$ and $y'(t)$. That is implicit differentiation with a new independent variable. The geometry is unchanged. A moving point is required to stay on the curve, so its velocity is tangent to the curve.

How to practice

  1. Differentiate both sides before you isolate anything. Leave $y'$ visible.
  2. Solve for $y'$. Substitute the point only at the end.
  3. Check that the point satisfies the original equation.
  4. Ask whether $y'$ is infinite or undefined. If it is, sketch or switch the independent variable.
  5. When you can solve explicitly on a branch, compare once. Then trust the implicit path on equations you cannot solve.

A numerical slope check is possible if you can sample the curve. The numerical derivative calculator needs an explicit $f(x)$, so it is a comparison tool after you solve a branch, not a replacement for the implicit step.
