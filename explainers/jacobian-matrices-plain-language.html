Jacobian matrices in plain language

Explainer · Educational only · Updated 17 Sep 2026

A derivative in one variable is a single number, the best linear multiplier near a point. When a map sends several inputs to several outputs, the best linear multiplier is a matrix. That matrix is the Jacobian. It is not a new kind of derivative. It is the derivative, written so that every first partial derivative has a seat.

If you already like the sentence $f(x)\approx f(a)+f'(a)(x-a)$, the multivariable cousin is

$$\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{a}+\mathbf{h})\approx \mathbf{f}(\mathbf{a})+D\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{a})\,\mathbf{h}.$$

Here $D\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{a})$ is the Jacobian matrix at $\mathbf{a}$, and $\mathbf{h}$ is a small input step. Matrix times vector is the linear map. For the geometry of those maps, keep linear algebra nearby. For the calculus setting, use the multivariable pillar.

How the entries are arranged

Write $\mathbf{f}(x,y)=(u(x,y),v(x,y))$ as a map from the plane to the plane. The Jacobian is

$$D\mathbf{f}=\begin{pmatrix} u_x & u_y \\ v_x & v_y \end{pmatrix}.$$

Row $i$ is the gradient of the $i$th output. Column $j$ is the velocity of the output when you move only the $j$th input. Both readings are useful. Gradients tell you how each coordinate function tilts. Columns tell you where the standard basis arrows go after the linear approximation.

That column picture is the same story as “a matrix is the images of basis vectors,” which also appears in eigenvalues in plain language. The Jacobian is the matrix of the derivative map, not a mysterious grid of extra symbols.

A polar example you can picture

Example Polar coordinates send $(r,\theta)$ to $(x,y)=(r\cos\theta,\, r\sin\theta)$. The Jacobian is

$$D=\begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta & -r\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & r\cos\theta \end{pmatrix}.$$

The first column is the unit radial direction. Increasing $r$ a little moves you straight out from the origin. The second column is a tangential direction of length $r$. Increasing $\theta$ a little walks you along a circle, and a farther radius makes the same angle change sweep a longer arc. The determinant is $r$. Local area in the $(r,\theta)$ plane is scaled by $|r|$ when you look in the $(x,y)$ plane. That is why polar integrals pick up a factor of $r$.

At $r=0$ the determinant vanishes. Many angles collapse to one point. The linear map is singular, which matches the picture. Polar coordinates are a poor chart exactly at the origin.

What the determinant is saying

For a map from $\mathbb{R}^n$ to $\mathbb{R}^n$, $\det D\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{a})$ is the signed volume scaling of the best linear approximation at $\mathbf{a}$. Positive means orientation is kept. Negative means it is flipped. Zero means some direction is crushed, so the map is not locally invertible.

The inverse function theorem, in undergraduate language, says that if $D\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{a})$ is invertible, then $\mathbf{f}$ itself is invertible on small neighborhoods, and the derivative of the inverse is the inverse matrix of the derivative. You do not need the full proof to use the slogan. A nonzero Jacobian determinant is a green light for a local inverse. A zero determinant is a warning, not an automatic disaster everywhere, but it is a place to slow down.

Try the 2 by 2 matrix toolkit on a Jacobian you have computed by hand. Check the determinant and, when it is safe, the inverse. The 2 by 2 eigenvalue tool can show the stretch factors of that linear map along special directions. Those numbers describe the approximation, not the full nonlinear map.

Maps that change dimension

If $\mathbf{f}:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}^m$, the Jacobian is an $m\times n$ matrix. It is still the derivative. It just cannot be square, so it has no single determinant. Rank becomes the useful word. Full rank means the linear approximation hits a subspace of the largest possible dimension. For a scalar function, $m=1$, the Jacobian is a single row, which is the gradient. The linear approximation is the tangent plane story from multivariable calculus.

For a curve $\mathbf{r}(t)$ in space, $n=1$, the Jacobian is a single column $\mathbf{r}'(t)$, the velocity. Nothing here conflicts. The same definition specializes cleanly.

Change of variables in integrals

When you rewrite a double or triple integral in new coordinates, you are pushing a region through a map $\mathbf{g}$. Little parallelepipeds in the parameter domain become little parallelepipeds in space. Their volumes differ by $|\det D\mathbf{g}|$. That factor is the Jacobian determinant, taken in absolute value because volume is unsigned in the usual integral. Forgetting it is the most common computational error after a substitution looks “correct” on the integrand alone.

Pitfall. The Jacobian matrix and the Jacobian determinant are related and not identical. People say “the Jacobian” for either. In a sentence about invertibility or eigenvalues you want the matrix. In a sentence about $dA$ or $dV$ after a substitution you want the absolute value of the determinant.

How this sits next to other ideas

Constrained optimization uses gradients of a function and of a constraint. Those gradients are Jacobian rows. See Lagrange multipliers intuition. Vector calculus theorems package derivatives of fields. The derivative of a field is built from partials that could be arranged as a Jacobian. Stokes theorem intuition is one of those theorems.

If you want a numerical feel for a single partial, hold every variable but one fixed and use the numerical derivative calculator on that slice. The vector operations tool helps you check orthogonality when a column of $D\mathbf{f}$ should be tangent to a circle or a sphere.
