AP Calculus AB study pathway

Pathway · Educational only · Not affiliated with College Board · Updated 22 Sep 2026

This pathway is a study map for readers who already have AP Calculus AB skills and want those skills to mean something. It orders topic pages, explainers, calculators, and glossary entries that already live here. It is not an official review course, and it is not affiliated with College Board.

Who this pathway is for

Start here if you can differentiate and integrate common functions and you want a calmer story before college calculus, series, or a first differential equation. You might be between an AB course and the next class, or studying on your own with rules that never say what the symbols measure.

Many AB courses stop before series, so those steps come after the core ideas. If you are only repairing limits, derivatives, and integrals, pause after the integral step and return.

Prerequisites

You need algebra, graphs, and enough comfort with limits to talk about nearby values. Derivative rules and a picture of the definite integral as signed area are assumed.

If those feel shaky, begin with calculus foundations and the glossary entries for limit, derivative, and definite integral. Return when you can say, in one sentence each, what those three words ask. OpenStax Calculus and MIT OpenCourseWare are on the sources page if you want a full book beside this map.

What you will be able to do

When you finish the core, you should be able to say what a limit, a derivative, and a definite integral measure, not only how to compute them. You should estimate a slope and a limit numerically and know what those numbers are not. You should find a slope from an implicit equation, tell a sequence from a series, name a convergence test including when it stays silent, and separate a simple first order equation without losing a solution you divided away.

Suggested order

Work in this order. One step per sitting is enough.

  1. Read calculus foundations as a map of change and accumulation.
  2. Read what a derivative actually measures until you can explain the derivative at a point, with units, to someone who knows slope but not the notation.
  3. Open the numerical derivative calculator on a function you can differentiate by hand. Compare one estimate with your exact slope. If they disagree by a lot, change the step size before you blame the algebra.
  4. Open the numerical limit estimator beside the glossary entries for limit and continuity. Try a hole and a jump. A table can show settling or a refusal to settle. It does not prove a limit. See the FAQ on calculators versus understanding.
  5. Read epsilon delta intuition without terror and the epsilon delta definition. Optional on a first pass. Worth it if a later course will ask for proofs: the tolerance on outputs comes first, then a window on inputs. If this is the step you care about, continue on intro to proofs.
  6. Read implicit differentiation intuition, then return to the numerical derivative calculator on a branch you can solve. For $x^{2}+y^{2}=1$, the slope is $-x/y$ when $y$ is not zero. On the upper half, compare that value at $x=0.5$ with the numerical slope of the square root of $1-x^{2}$. The glossary line for implicit differentiation is the short reminder.
  7. Use the Riemann sum calculator with the glossary entry for definite integral. For an increasing positive function, a left sum undershoots and a right sum overshoots. Say which is which before you run a modest number of rectangles.
  8. Read sequences, series, and when infinite sums make sense. Use the arithmetic and geometric sequence calculator for a finite geometric sum you can also do by hand. It does not decide infinite convergence. Keep sequence, series, partial sum, and convergence distinct.
  9. Read convergence tests for series without fog with the comparison test, the ratio test, and the root test. For each test, write the hypothesis and the case where it gives no decision.
  10. Read differential equations first look and the separable differential equations walkthrough. The glossary entries for differential equation and initial value problem name the objects.

Checkpoints and practice habits

Do these on paper. A calculator check comes after a sentence you can say, not instead of it.

Practice habit. For a derivative you already know how to compute, write one sentence naming the quantity whose rate you are computing. Distance, cost, population, or temperature is enough. Include units. “The derivative is 3” is not the sentence. “Volume is increasing by 3 liters per minute at this time” is the sentence.

Slope check. The derivative of $x^{2}$ at $x=4$ is $8$. Ask the numerical derivative calculator for an estimate. Record a step size you trust and one that looked worse.

Series check. In one sentence, state how a sequence differs from a series, and which object the ratio test is about. If you never mention adding terms, reread sequences, series, and when infinite sums make sense.

Differential equation check. Separate $dy/dx=2xy$, and treat $y=0$ on its own. You should reach $y=Ae^{x^{2}}$ for a nonzero constant $A$, because dividing by $y$ hid the zero solution. Differentiate a nonzero answer and confirm that you recover $2xy$. An initial condition selects a single $A$. That is an initial value problem.

Common pitfalls for self learners

Speed with the power rule can hide a missing definition. If what a derivative actually measures feels slow, the pathway is doing its job. A calculator output is not a proof and not an algebraic derivative.

Pitfall. The ratio test is silent when the limit of successive absolute ratios is $1$. Silence is not convergence and not divergence. Switch tests, as convergence tests for series without fog says. Separating variables has a cousin of that mistake: if you divide by $y$, you still owe $y=0$ a look.

If the next course includes series, do the sequence and test steps before you need them. Skipping them because an AB course omitted them makes the next course feel like a new language.

Related reading and the next pathway

If epsilon delta was the interesting part, continue with intro to proofs. If several variables are next, take the linear algebra first course first. Jacobian matrices in plain language is the handoff, and the multivariable intro comes after it. If models of change were the interesting part, stay with differential equations first look. A slope field is the picture. Lagrange multipliers and Stokes theorem wait until you are studying several variables. Compare routes on the pathways hub.

Start with the topic map if the rules feel rusty. If computation is already easy and meaning is the gap, open what a derivative actually measures.

Common questions

Is this an AP Calculus AB exam review?

No. The map assumes AP Calculus AB skills and aims at meaning, series, and a first differential equation. It is not affiliated with College Board and does not replace official exam materials or a course.

Do I need series if my AB course skipped them?

If the next course includes series, yes. Read the sequences explainer and the convergence tests explainer. If you are only repairing limits, derivatives, and integrals, pause after the Riemann sum step and return later.

Should I study epsilon delta on this pathway?

It is optional on a first pass and worth the time if a later course will ask for proofs. Read it for the quantifier order, then continue. The intro to proofs pathway is where you practice writing the arguments.

When should I open a calculator?

After you can say what the number means. The numerical derivative estimates a slope. The limit estimator samples nearby values. The Riemann sum calculator draws accumulation. The sequence calculator adds finitely many terms. None of them replaces an algebraic derivative or a convergence proof.

What should I study after this pathway?

If proofs pulled you in, use intro to proofs. If several variables are next, use the linear algebra first course, then the Jacobian explainer. If models of change pulled you in, stay with the differential equations topic and the separable walkthrough.
